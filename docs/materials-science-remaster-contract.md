# Materials Science Remaster Contract

## Identity and rotation role

Materials Science is an Applied Science navigation hub and physical-evidence lesson. It follows Data Science to change academic world, visual grammar, primary model, and assessment language. The page should feel like a specimen and test bench: a learner moves between processing history, internal structure, measured response, service performance, and failure.

The page must not treat a material name as a complete specification, present schematic microstructures as microscope records, equate one mechanical property with overall quality, or provide unsupported material-selection advice.

## Bounded direct-child ontology

Materials Science owns eight direct branches organized into two visible tracks.

Cross-cutting lenses:

1. Structure & Bonding
2. Material Properties
3. Processing & Characterization

Material families:

4. Metals & Alloys
5. Ceramics & Glasses
6. Polymers
7. Composites
8. Electronic & Functional Materials

All eight are planned, non-clickable units until substantive pages exist. The two-track atlas is the first major section after the header. It states that the tracks are coordinated ways to enter the field rather than a single exhaustive taxonomy. Individual alloys, ceramics, polymers, tests, processes, failure modes, devices, and applications belong inside these branches before another direct peer is proposed.

## Overview lesson flow

The root follows a Navigate → Relate → Model → Select → Check sequence:

1. navigate three cross-cutting lenses and five material families;
2. relate processing → structure → properties → performance;
3. model three idealized normalized mechanical-response shapes;
4. translate function, environment, manufacture, failure, repair, and lifecycle into selection questions;
5. assess units, curve evidence, processing causality, and service boundaries.

The root does not attempt to survey every material, teach every characterization method, replace a design code, or recommend a material for a real component.

## Deterministic model contract

One shared module owns exact branch parity, three mechanical-response profiles, curve generation, regime classification, engineering-stress arithmetic, and assessment fixtures.

The mechanical instrument uses local normalized equations:

- brittle-like: a steep linear cue through 2% strain, followed by a teaching fracture state;
- ductile-like: an initial linear cue, an idealized yielding/plastic region, and a teaching fracture state beyond 20%;
- elastomer-like: a nonlinear reversible-response cue through the 20% teaching window.

The curves are shape models, not measured property data. They do not imply that every ceramic is brittle, every metal ductile, or every polymer elastomeric. Actual response depends on composition, processing, microstructure, geometry, test method, temperature, rate, environment, and direction.

The server-rendered background shows a schematic tensile frame, gauge length, force arrows, crack cue, microscope window, grains, lattice points, defects, and the four-part relationship rail without random values, viewport measurement, canvas, or a client-only first frame.

## Assessment contract

The specimen-review lab contains four deterministic files:

- calculate `18,000 N ÷ 60 mm² = 300 MPa` engineering stress;
- infer stiffness from initial elastic slope without silently claiming strength or toughness;
- trace heat treatment through microstructure to measured mechanical response;
- reject room-temperature tensile strength as sufficient evidence for long-duration cyclic service at 700 °C in a corrosive atmosphere.

Every file supports unanswered, correct, incorrect, changed-answer, switched-file, and reset states. Feedback reconstructs the unit or test boundary rather than merely announcing a score.

## Vocabulary ownership

Materials Science owns twenty root terms: microstructure, crystal structure, amorphous, phase, grain, grain boundary, crystal defect, dislocation, processing, characterization, stiffness, strength, ductility, toughness, fracture, fatigue, creep, corrosion, composite, and anisotropy.

The terms register at `applied.materials-science` and aggregate into the Applied Science drawer through curriculum containment. As child units are built, narrower terms should move to their owning child registrations while remaining inherited by this parent.

## Source and model boundary

The processing–structure–property relationship is checked against the NIST Materials Design Toolkit. Characterization context is checked against NIST's X-Ray Topography guide. The service sensitivity of mechanical data is grounded by NIST mechanical-testing programs that measure response under specified loading, temperature, strain-rate, geometry, and instrument conditions.

The specimen, microstructure, force, curve, and service diagrams on this page are local schematic teaching fixtures. They are not empirical records, standards, acceptance criteria, or material certificates.
