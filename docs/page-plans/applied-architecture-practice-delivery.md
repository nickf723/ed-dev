# Practice, Codes & Delivery Unit Plan

## Identity

**Route:** `/applied-science/architecture/practice-delivery`  
**Parent:** Architecture  
**Page type:** Unit

## Learning contract

**Learner goal:** understand that architecture is delivered through a network of requirements, documents, participants, decisions, money, time, contracts, reviews, and field changes, not simply by finishing a design drawing.

**Primary mental model:** project information moves through a sequence of commitments. A requirement becomes design information, that information is coordinated and documented, contractual/project-delivery structures determine who acts on it, construction produces new questions and changes, and final verification/closeout records what was delivered.

**Evidence of understanding:** the learner can identify what kind of project process is involved when a problem concerns a regulatory requirement, drawing/detail, consultant conflict, budget/schedule pressure, delivery relationship, or field question.

## Scope boundary

Belongs here:

- categories of regulatory authority and approval
- architectural documentation and cross-reference logic
- multidisciplinary coordination
- qualitative cost and schedule relationships
- procurement / delivery organization
- construction-phase information loops and closeout

Does not belong here:

- jurisdiction-specific legal advice
- licensure-exam coaching as the unit's purpose
- contract interpretation for a real project
- real cost estimates without location/market/scope data
- code-compliance verdicts from generic examples
- contractor safety or means-and-methods instruction

## Atomic lesson sequence

1. **Regulatory Frameworks & Approvals** — different authorities and requirement types act on the same project
2. **Documentation & Drawing Sets** — one building condition is represented at multiple scales and in multiple documents
3. **Consultants & Coordination** — reconcile shared geometry, requirements, and responsibilities across disciplines
4. **Cost & Schedule** — design choices affect quantity, complexity, uncertainty, sequencing, lead time, and project pressure
5. **Procurement & Delivery Methods** — compare how roles, contracts, design, and construction relationships can be organized
6. **Construction Administration & Closeout** — field questions, submittals, observations, changes, deficiencies, commissioning, and record information form feedback loops

## Unit representation

Primary representation: a project-information flow rather than a fake project-management dashboard.

The same sample design issue should be traceable through several stages:

**requirement → design decision → consultant coordination → document → procurement/commitment → field question/change → verified/recorded outcome**

This makes process visible without implying every real project follows one identical contractual workflow.

## Regulatory guardrail

Codes, zoning, accessibility rules, permits, fire review, environmental requirements, preservation review, and other authorities vary substantially by jurisdiction and project type. Examples must always be labeled illustrative and should teach categories and relationships rather than generic compliance rules.

## Cost/schedule guardrail

Never create a fake price oracle. A useful educational model can show pressures such as:

- more quantity tends to add cost
- unusual complexity may increase uncertainty and coordination
- long-lead items can affect schedule
- phased work changes sequence and logistics
- late changes can have different consequences than early changes

But real estimates require actual scope, quantities, location, market, labor, procurement, risk, and time data.

## Cross-disciplinary boundary

This unit explains the architect's project-coordination context. It should acknowledge owners, contractors, consultants, authorities, and specialists without implying architects unilaterally control every decision or responsibility.

## Definition of done

The unit is ready when the learner can explain:

> A building is delivered by coordinated information and commitments over time. Design quality depends not only on the idea, but on whether requirements are understood, documents agree, participants coordinate, resources are managed, and field changes are resolved coherently.
