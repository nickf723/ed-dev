# Site & Context Unit Plan

## Identity

**Page:** Site & Context  
**Route:** `/applied-science/architecture/site-context`  
**Parent:** Architecture  
**Page type:** Unit

## Unit throughline

**observe → map → interpret → respond**

The unit teaches that a site is an active field of existing conditions and directional forces rather than an empty plot awaiting a building.

## Learning contract

A learner should understand that architecture responds to site conditions only after those conditions are observed and represented clearly. The same program can lead to different architecture because climate, terrain, water, access, neighbors, infrastructure, and other site conditions differ.

## Child lessons

1. **Site Analysis & Constraints** — what existing conditions and boundaries already shape the project?
2. **Climate & Orientation** — how do sun, shade, wind, exposure, and seasonal patterns create directional opportunities and risks?
3. **Topography & Water** — how do contours, slope, ground contact, drainage, and runoff affect placement and grading?
4. **Access & Surroundings** — how do pedestrian, vehicle, service, street, neighbor, view, noise, and public-realm relationships shape arrival and edges?

## Unit-page responsibility

The unit page organizes these site-reading lenses around one layered site diagram. It should not become a full GIS, solar-analysis, grading, or traffic tool.

## Primary representation

A composite site drawing in which property/study boundary, topography, water, climate, vegetation, access, street edge, and neighboring context occupy the same map.

The learner should see that these layers can reinforce or conflict with one another.

## Key distinctions

- Existing condition is a fact; opportunity or constraint is an interpretation.
- Site forces are often directional rather than uniform.
- Context includes environmental, physical, infrastructural, legal, social, and spatial conditions, but detailed legal/jurisdictional requirements belong in sourced future content.
- A site response is a design decision, not something the raw data determines automatically.

## Navigation

**Parent:** Architecture  
**Children:** four atomic lessons listed above, initially planned until their routes are built.

## Visual identity

Use the Architecture blueprint language but shift toward survey/map graphics: contours, property lines, grade, vegetation, water paths, arrows, street edges, and neighboring masses.

## Definition of done

The unit is successful when:

- the layered site diagram clarifies why the four child lessons exist
- planned child lessons are visible but non-clickable
- no single layer is mistaken for the whole site problem
- observation and interpretation remain conceptually separate
- the page feels like a unit map rather than a site-analysis dashboard
- the diagram remains legible at desktop and narrower widths
