# Topography & Water Page Plan

## Identity

**Route:** `/applied-science/architecture/site-context/topography-water`  
**Parent:** Site & Context  
**Page type:** Atomic lesson

## Learning contract

**Learner goal:** read simplified contour patterns as evidence of three-dimensional ground form and reason qualitatively about downhill water movement.

**Primary mental model:** contour lines connect equal elevations. Their spacing describes how quickly elevation changes across plan distance, while their bends reveal forms such as swales and ridges.

**Evidence of understanding:** the learner can distinguish a simplified slope, swale/valley, and ridge/spur from their contour shapes and predict whether water will generally collect or shed.

## Scope

Belongs here:

- equal-elevation meaning of contour lines
- relative steepness from contour spacing
- idealized uniform slope
- swale/valley contour pattern
- ridge/spur contour pattern
- qualitative downhill drainage behavior
- relationship between existing grade and later grading decisions

Does not belong here:

- parcel-specific topographic data
- detailed civil grading design
- stormwater sizing
- floodplain determination
- soil infiltration calculations
- drainage-code compliance
- a full hydrologic simulation

## Primary instrument

A contour studio with three canonical terrain states:

1. Uniform slope
2. Swale / valley
3. Ridge / spur

A spacing control changes the visual distance between contours while preserving the idea that the contour interval itself is conceptual and fixed.

The diagram should show:

- closer versus wider spacing
- direction of general downhill flow
- concentration of water along a swale
- splitting/shedding of water from a ridge

## Guardrails

- teaching elevations are arbitrary, not survey data
- actual slope requires real elevation and horizontal-distance measurements
- actual runoff depends on microtopography, soils, vegetation, surface cover, rainfall, drainage infrastructure, obstructions, and downstream conditions
- water does not generally flow along an equal-elevation contour
- proposed grading becomes a coordinated design involving accessibility, structure, landscape, utilities, drainage, and construction

## Visual verification

Check that:

- all three terrain forms are visually distinct
- swale V-shapes point uphill and ridge/spur V-shapes point downhill
- water arrows do not cover contour labels
- extreme spacing states remain readable
- arbitrary elevation labels look illustrative rather than authoritative
- the diagram does not imply a complete drainage analysis
